Creating a Cleaning Checklist for Your Home

A cleaning checklist is not instructions for how to clean. It is a written description of what the arrangement covers, so that the household and the person doing the work mean the same thing by it. This covers how to write one that is specific enough to be fair and short enough to be used — and it names no products or methods, because those are not ours to advise on.

What households should know

Four Rules for a List That Gets Used

Most checklists fail in one of four ways. Each has a straightforward fix.

1 · Write it room by room, not as categories

"Dusting" as a line item is a category and means whatever the reader decides. "Bedroom: window sills, shelf tops, fan" is a scope. Walking the house with a piece of paper and writing down what you actually see is far more effective than composing the list at a table, because you will notice the things you have stopped seeing.

2 · Separate every visit from periodic

Almost every household has a few things that need doing each time and a longer tail of things that need doing occasionally. Putting them all in one undifferentiated list means the occasional ones quietly never happen, and both sides eventually notice. Two short lists, with the periodic one carrying a rough interval, work much better than one long one.

3 · Be specific enough to be fair to both sides

A written list is not bureaucracy — it protects the person doing the work at least as much as the household. Vagueness always resolves in someone's favour later, and whichever way it goes, the other side feels the arrangement changed on them. Specific means each side would describe the item the same way, not that every motion is prescribed.

4 · Keep it honest about the time available

The most common failure is a list that could not be completed by anyone in the hours agreed. It is worth asking directly whether the scope is realistic, and taking an honest "not in that time" as useful information rather than as a problem. A shorter list done properly beats a longer one done partially, every time.

Practical considerations

What Belongs on the List

Scope only. This describes what is covered, never how anything should be cleaned, with what, or to what standard of hygiene.

Which rooms are in and out

Name them explicitly, including any that are outside the arrangement. Both sides then know exactly what the visit covers and nobody is left guessing.

The surfaces that get missed

Every home has them — the tops of things, behind things, the areas nobody looks at. If they matter to you, name them; if they do not, leave them off and say so.

Frequency against each item

Every visit, weekly, monthly, or occasionally. This single column removes most disagreements about whether something has been neglected.

What is explicitly excluded

Just as useful as what is included. Anything the household handles itself, or that needs a specialist, should be written down as out of scope.

Anything fragile or particular

Items to be handled carefully or left alone. Better recorded on the list than remembered after a breakage.

A review date

Agree when you will both look at the list again — a few weeks in is usually right. Naming it in advance makes revising it routine rather than a complaint.

Common questions

Questions Households Ask Us

How detailed should a cleaning checklist be?

Detailed enough that both sides would describe the job the same way, and no longer. Room by room and surface by surface works; a list so long nobody reads it does not. If an item is obvious to everyone, it does not need writing down.

Should everything be done on every visit?

Usually not, and pretending otherwise is how occasional tasks quietly never happen. Split the list into what happens every visit and what happens periodically, and name when the periodic items are due so they are not left to memory.

What if the list turns out to be too long for the time?

Then it is a wish list rather than a checklist, and it should be shortened or the time extended. A list nobody can complete leaves both sides feeling the arrangement is failing. Ask directly whether the scope is realistic and take an honest no seriously.

How often should we revise the checklist?

Review it after the first few weeks, once you can both see what the home actually needs, and then only when something changes — a season, a room's use, or a household member. Constant revision is as unsettling as never revising it at all.

Describe it in your own words — how many rooms, what needs doing, and how often. We will tell you honestly whether it is realistic in the hours you have in mind, and if we are not the right people for it, we will say so.
