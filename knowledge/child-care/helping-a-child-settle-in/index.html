Helping Children Adjust to a New Caregiver

This is about how a family structures the first weeks of an arrangement — the overlap, the routine, the handover, the review. It is not about child psychology, and it takes no view on how your child feels or should feel. If you are worried about how your child is coping, that is a conversation for your paediatrician, not for a page written by a placement agency.

When this is useful

In the First Weeks of a New Arrangement

Written for the parent organising the transition. It covers what the household can control — the shape of the first weeks — and nothing beyond that.

  • A caregiver is about to start and you want to plan the transition
  • The first few days have been bumpy and you are wondering what is normal
  • You are returning to work and the handover has to happen quickly
  • A caregiver is being replaced and you want the second transition to go better

What families should know

What the Household Can Actually Control

Not much of a transition is in a family's hands. These four things are, and they are worth spending the effort on.

Overlap rather than handover

The single most useful thing a household can arrange is time when the parent and the caregiver are both there and working alongside each other. It lets the caregiver learn the routine in context rather than from a page, lets you see how they work, and means the child is not meeting someone new and being left with them within the same hour. Two or three days is better than one; one is much better than none.

Change one thing at a time

Where you can, keep the routine steady while the person is new. A household that simultaneously introduces a caregiver, moves bedtime and starts a new nursery has changed several things at once and will not be able to tell which part is causing difficulty. It also gives the caregiver a clear structure to work to rather than a moving one.

Give the caregiver enough to work with

What your child likes, what they find difficult, the words your household uses for things, what usually helps at the end of a tiring afternoon. Not instructions on how to raise your child — just the accumulated household knowledge that would otherwise take a caregiver weeks to piece together. Preparing your home covers the written part of this.

Build in a review, and be honest at it

Agree in advance that you will look at how it is going at around two weeks and again at six. Naming the review in advance makes it much easier to raise something small, because doing so is expected rather than a complaint. Most difficulties at that stage are about routine or communication and are fixable in a conversation.

Practical considerations

Structuring the First Weeks

Practical arrangements a household can make. Nothing here is an interpretation of a child's behaviour or feelings.

A short introduction first

A brief meeting before the start date, with you present, so the first day is not also the first time your child has seen this person.

Consistent start and finish times

Predictable timings in the first fortnight give the day a shape. Variable hours are harder for a household to absorb while everything else is new.

Say goodbye rather than slipping out

Families widely find that leaving without saying anything makes subsequent departures harder. A brief, matter-of-fact goodbye is what most households settle on.

A short daily update

A few lines at the end of the day — what was eaten, what was done, anything worth mentioning. It is how you find out about small things before they become large ones.

Back the caregiver in front of the child

Where you disagree with something, raise it privately rather than in front of your child. Undermining a caregiver in the moment makes the arrangement harder for everyone in it.

Keep the same person where you can

Familiarity accumulates. Frequent substitution resets what the caregiver knows about your household and restarts the settling period each time.

General guidance vs our service

Where This Guidance Ends and Our Service Begins

Everything above is general and holds whoever you arrange care through. This is the narrow part the care we actually provide is responsible for.

What Quality Care can help with

  • Arranging an introduction before the start date
  • Briefing the caregiver on your routine and household knowledge before day one
  • Setting up a daily update so you hear how the day went
  • Protecting continuity — keeping the same person where the arrangement allows
  • Reviewing with you, and changing the placement where the fit is genuinely wrong

What we do NOT advise on

  • How a child is coping emotionally. We are not qualified to assess it and we will not offer reassurance about it
  • Separation, attachment, distress, or any psychological interpretation of behaviour
  • Whether a reaction is normal for a child's age — that is a question for your paediatrician
  • Developmental milestones or behavioural assessment
  • Discipline methods, parenting philosophy, or how you should respond to your child
  • Child health, illness or medication

This page describes how to organise a placement, not how a child experiences one. We are a placement service, not a medical or child-development provider. If you are at all worried about how your child is coping, or about their behaviour, development or health, speak to your paediatrician or a qualified professional who knows the child. If it is an emergency, seek immediate medical help.

Common questions

Questions Families Ask Us

How many overlap days should we plan?

Whatever you can realistically arrange, and more is generally better than fewer. Families who manage two or three days at home while the caregiver works alongside them report an easier start than families who hand over at the door on the first morning.

Should we keep the routine exactly the same at first?

Keeping the routine steady while the person is new means only one thing has changed rather than several at once. It also gives the caregiver a clear structure to work to. Changes to the routine itself are easier once the arrangement has bedded in.

How long does settling usually take?

It varies so much by child and household that we would rather not give a number. What we can say is that most families review at around the two-week mark and again at six weeks, and that a first week which felt difficult is not on its own a reason to conclude the arrangement is wrong.

What if we are worried about how our child is coping?

Speak to your paediatrician. Questions about how a child is coping emotionally are outside what we can advise on, and we would rather say so plainly than offer reassurance we are not qualified to give. Separately, tell us if you think the placement itself is the problem.

Tell Us How It Is Going

If a placement is not settling and you think the arrangement is the reason, tell us early. Most of it is fixable in a conversation, and where it is not, changing the person is the right answer rather than persevering.
