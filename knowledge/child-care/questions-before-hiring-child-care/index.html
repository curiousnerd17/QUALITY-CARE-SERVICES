Questions to Ask Before Hiring a Child Caregiver

Someone will be alone in your home with a child who may be too young to tell you how the day went. That is the placement parents think hardest about, and it deserves more than a phone call. These are the questions worth asking, what to verify, and the answers that should prompt a second question.

When this is useful

Before You Agree to Anyone Starting

A general checklist that applies however you arrange care — through an agency, a recommendation, or privately. If you have already chosen someone, preparing the home is the next step.

  • You are arranging child care at home for the first time
  • Someone has been proposed and you want to check properly before agreeing
  • You are comparing two options and cannot tell them apart
  • A previous arrangement ended badly and you want to choose differently

What families should know

Four Groups of Questions

Ask across all four. Parents who only ask about experience are the ones most often surprised later, because experience is the easiest thing to claim and the hardest to check on the phone.

1 · Verification — before anything else

These are yes-or-no questions and should get yes-or-no answers: has identity been verified, and against what document; has the current address been verified; has a background check been completed, and when; who is accountable if something goes wrong during the placement. This is the service where families are trusting most, and it is the one where a vague answer matters most. If verification is described as "usually done" or "in progress", treat that as a complete answer — it has not been done.

2 · Experience with children of this age

Looking after a two-year-old and looking after a nine-year-old are different jobs. Ask for a specific example at your child's age: what the days looked like, what was hardest, what they did about it. Concrete answers describe a particular household; general answers about loving children describe a category, and the difference is usually clear within a minute.

3 · The practical arrangement

Agree these before the first day rather than in week three: exactly which hours; whether the role includes school runs, meals or tidying, and whether it does not; what happens on a day the caregiver cannot come; who is permitted to collect your child; and how the arrangement can be ended by either side. Most difficulty in this placement is scope or authority left unstated.

4 · What they ask you

The most revealing question and the one parents rarely think of. Someone who will do this work well wants to know about your child — the routine, what they can already do themselves, what upsets them, what settles them, what your house rules are. If nobody asks you anything, nobody is planning to fit your household.

Practical considerations

Answers Worth a Second Question

None of these means someone is dishonest. All of them mean ask once more before agreeing.

"We can start tomorrow, no paperwork"

Speed is appealing when you are due back at work. But verification takes time by definition, and this is the last placement where you want it skipped.

Talking about your child rather than to them

In an introduction, watch whether the caregiver engages your child directly and at their level. Someone who talks past a child in a first meeting will do it every day afterwards.

Reluctance to let you meet first

You are entitled to a conversation, and to a short meeting with your child present. Any reasonable arrangement makes room for that even on a tight timeline.

Opinions offered about your child

Someone volunteering views on your child's development, behaviour or health from a first meeting is working well outside their role. Those questions belong with your paediatrician.

Vagueness about accountability

"We will sort it out" is not an answer. You want to know who to call, in what timeframe, and what happens if a replacement is needed at short notice.

Disagreement with your house rules

If your stated rules on screen time, snacks or outings are met with resistance in the interview, that will not improve once the placement starts. Better to find out now.

General guidance vs our service

Where This Guidance Ends and Our Service Begins

Everything above applies however you hire. This is what we do when we place a caregiver with a child at home — and you are entitled to put every question above to us.

What Quality Care can help with

  • Identity, address and background verification, completed before anyone is placed with a child
  • Matching on temperament and experience relevant to your child's age
  • Arranging an introduction before anything is agreed
  • Settling hours, scope and collection arrangements in advance with both sides
  • Changing the placement where the fit is wrong, rather than leaving it to run

What we do NOT advise on

  • Parenting. Your rules are yours. We describe how to brief a caregiver, never what to decide
  • Child health, illness, medication or feeding plans
  • Developmental milestones, or whether a child is ahead or behind on any of them
  • Behavioural or psychological assessment, of a child or of a caregiver
  • Discipline methods or any parenting philosophy
  • Guaranteeing an outcome. We can describe how we verify and match; we cannot promise how a relationship will go

We arrange practical care at home; we are not a medical or child-development provider. For your child's health, development, feeding or behaviour, speak to your paediatrician or a qualified professional who knows the child. If it is an emergency, seek immediate medical help.

Common questions

Questions Families Ask Us

What verification should be complete before someone is alone with our child?

Identity proof, address proof and background checks, all completed before the first day rather than during the first week. Ask specifically what was checked and when. An arrangement where verification is described as in progress is one where verification has not happened.

Should we meet the caregiver with our child present?

A short meeting with your child there, after you have spoken to the person alone first, tells you a great deal. You are watching whether they engage with your child directly and at their level, rather than talking only to you about them.

How much does formal qualification matter for this role?

Less than experience with children of your child's age and a calm, patient manner. Where a household needs something specific — a particular language, help with school work, experience with a condition your doctor has told you about — say so and treat it as a requirement.

What should we ask about emergencies?

Ask what they would do and who they would call, and make sure your own written instructions are unambiguous: your numbers, a second contact, your child's doctor. What matters is that the caregiver contacts you and follows medical advice, not that they make judgements of their own.
