Preparing Your Home Before a Maid Starts Work

Almost every difficulty in a household arrangement traces back to something nobody wrote down or showed. An hour of preparation before the first day prevents most of it. This covers the task list, the walk-through, and the practical things worth settling while everyone is still being polite about them.

When this is useful

In the Days Before Someone Starts

This assumes an arrangement has been agreed. If you are still choosing, how to choose a maid is the earlier step.

  • A start date is set and you want the first week to go smoothly
  • This is the first time you have had regular household help
  • A previous arrangement drifted into disagreement about what the job was
  • Someone new is replacing a person who had been with you a long time

What households should know

Write It Down, Then Walk It Through

Four things, in order. The first two take an hour between them and prevent most of what otherwise goes wrong.

1 · The task list, room by room

Not "cleaning" but what cleaning means in your home: which rooms, which surfaces, how often, and what is included in a normal day versus what is occasional. Write it down. A written list is not bureaucracy — it is the only version of the job that both sides can point at later, and it protects the person doing the work just as much as it protects the household.

2 · The walk-through on day one

Take twenty minutes and physically show the house. Where supplies are kept, which cloth is for which surface, how the machine works, what the household is particular about. Almost everything a new person gets wrong in the first fortnight is something nobody showed them, and it is far quicker to demonstrate once than to correct three times.

3 · Scope, stated as scope

If some rooms are outside the arrangement, or certain things are handled by the household, say so plainly at the start. Framed as scope, this is ordinary and unremarkable. Left unsaid and raised later, the same information sounds like an accusation. Clarity at the beginning is the version that treats everyone fairly.

4 · How you will talk to each other

Agree how the household raises something that is not right, and how the person raises something they need — a supply that has run out, a day they cannot come, a task that does not fit the time. Arrangements where only one direction of communication is established tend not to last. The hiring conversation is the natural place to settle this.

Practical considerations

The Practical Arrangements

Ordinary household logistics. Nothing here is contractual, and none of it concerns pay.

Supplies and equipment

Who provides them, who says when something has run out, and where they are kept. Usually the household provides — but say so rather than assume, and make restocking somebody's job.

Access and timings

How they get in, what happens if nobody is home, and whether the timing is fixed or has some flexibility. Decide this deliberately rather than improvising on the first morning.

Somewhere to keep their things

A place to leave a bag and a change of footwear, and somewhere to sit for a break. Small, easily overlooked, and it signals that the arrangement is a professional one.

Water and a break

For anything beyond a short visit, where they can get water and take a few minutes. Households that treat this as obvious keep people considerably longer than those that do not.

Which rooms are in the arrangement

Agreed at the start as a matter of scope. Both sides then know exactly what the job covers, and nobody is left guessing where they are expected to be.

A first-fortnight check-in

Agree in advance that you will both review how it is going after two weeks. Naming it early makes raising something small feel routine rather than like a complaint.

Common questions

Questions Households Ask Us

Who provides the cleaning supplies and equipment?

In most household arrangements the home provides them, but it is worth saying so explicitly rather than assuming. Agree who buys, who says when something has run out, and where everything is kept. It is a small conversation that removes a recurring irritation.

Should we be at home on the first day?

If you can, yes. A walk-through on the first morning — showing where things are kept and what the task list means room by room — saves far more time than it costs, and it is much harder to do over the phone afterwards.

How do we handle rooms or items we would rather were left alone?

Say so plainly at the start, as a matter of scope rather than suspicion. Being clear about which rooms are in the arrangement and which are not protects both sides — the household gets what it expects, and nobody is left guessing about where they should or should not be.

How should we raise it when something is not done properly?

Directly, early and privately, referring back to the agreed task list rather than to a general impression. Most shortfalls in the first weeks are misunderstandings about what a task meant, and they are straightforward to correct if raised before frustration accumulates.

Tell Us What Your Home Needs

Describe it in your own words — the size of the home, the work involved, and the timings that would suit. We will agree the task list with both sides before anyone starts, and if we are not the right people for it, we will say so.
