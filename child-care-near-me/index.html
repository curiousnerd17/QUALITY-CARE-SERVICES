The date behind the call

When You Are Due Back at Work

Speed is appealing when leave is ending. It is also the one pressure we will not let shorten the checks, because this is the last placement anybody should want rushed. What we can do is be clear about the window rather than optimistic about it.

  • The date you are actually due back, told to us on the first call rather than the third
  • Your child's age, because a narrower age band means a shorter list of suitable people
  • Whether the pattern is school hours, a full working day, or something that changes week to week
  • Whether a school pickup is part of it, which turns one location into two
  • Whether anyone else is at home during those hours

Getting to the house

What We Need in Order to Reach You

More than the address, and less than you might think.

The house, and the school if there is a pickup

Two locations rather than one. Naming the school early tells us more about whether an arrangement is workable than the home address does on its own.

A landmark either side

Something recognisable close to the house — and close to the school gate if the caregiver will be going there. Both journeys get made, so both are worth describing.

A location pin on WhatsApp

Quicker than typing directions, and it removes the ambiguity that colony names sometimes carry in Kota.

A contact who is reachable during working hours

The whole point of this arrangement is that you are at work. Give us someone who can answer a phone in the middle of the day, even if that is not you.

What happens next

After You Call or Message

Four steps, and none of them is skipped to hit a date.

1 · We ask about your child, then the hours

Age first, because it decides the shortlist. Then the pattern of the week and whether a school run is part of it.

2 · We match on the age band, not on a general claim

Being good with children is easy to say. We are looking for someone who has done this age, in a household like yours.

3 · The checks finish before you meet anyone

You see who is coming and what has been verified about them before the arrangement starts, not while it is running.

4 · A settling-in period, deliberately

Your child gets used to someone new at their own pace. Plan for it rather than expecting the first week to look like the fourth.

Being clear

What We Cannot Do at Short Notice

  • Start someone tomorrow with the paperwork unfinished, whatever the date on your leave letter says
  • Provide tuition, homework help or anything that is teaching rather than care
  • Offer developmental, behavioural or psychological assessment of a child
  • Promise how the relationship between your child and a caregiver will turn out

The first is a limit we impose on ourselves. If another agency offers to skip it, that is worth noticing rather than taking.

Worth thinking about

Good With Children Is the Easiest Thing to Claim

The risk here is not scarcity, it is a match that sounds right

There is no shortage of people who are good with children, which is exactly what makes this difficult. Looking after a two-year-old and looking after a nine-year-old are different jobs, and the difference does not show up on a phone call — experience is the easiest thing to claim and the hardest to check. Choosing on proximity means choosing on the one attribute that is trivially verifiable and least predictive. Tell us the age and the day, and let the shortlist be built on that.

Before anyone arrives

Everyone We Place Is Verified First

  • Identity — government photo ID is checked against the person in front of us, so nobody is placed on a recommendation alone
  • Address — address proof is verified, so we know where the person lives before they are sent to the address you give us
  • Background and references — both completed before a placement is confirmed, never alongside it
  • Experience matching — someone who has looked after a nine-year-old after school is not automatically the right person for a two-year-old at home all day
  • Replacement — if the fit turns out to be wrong once someone has started, arranging a replacement is our job, not yours

Verification lowers the risk of the wrong person reaching your home; it is not a clinical credential. How we verify the people we place explains the full process.

Common questions

Questions Parents Ask When Arranging This

The practical ones, mostly asked with a date already in mind.

I go back to work in ten days. How much of this can realistically be done in that time?

Often enough, but not always, and it depends on how narrow the requirement is. Tell us the date you are due back on the first call rather than later, because it changes what we prioritise. What we will not do is compress the checks to fit the date. Anyone offering to start tomorrow with no paperwork is telling you something about how they work, and this is the placement where that matters most.

Our home and my child's school are in different parts of Kota. Which address matters?

Both, and it is worth saying so early. If the arrangement includes a school pickup, the caregiver is working across two locations rather than one, and the practical journey is home to school to home rather than a single trip. Give us the area of the house and the area of the school together and we will tell you whether the pattern works.

Everyone says they are good with children. How do I check that for a child my child's age?

Ask for something concrete rather than a reassurance: how long, in what kind of household, and which parts of the day they actually handled. Someone who has run bedtime for a toddler and someone who has managed homework and an evening meal have both looked after children, and neither is interchangeable with the other. Tell us your child's age and what a normal day looks like, and we will match on that.

We tried someone before and my child did not take to them. Does starting again mean starting from zero?

For your child, largely yes — a new person restarts the period of getting used to someone, and that is worth planning for rather than hoping around. For us it does not, because what you learned from the first attempt is useful. Tell us what did not work. A specific complaint narrows the next shortlist far more than a general one.

The hours we need change in the school holidays. Is that a problem?

It is a normal thing to plan for rather than a problem, provided it is discussed when the arrangement is set up instead of the week the holidays start. A pattern that swings between a few hours a day and a full day is a different requirement from a steady one, and it is easier to find someone who has agreed to both in advance than to renegotiate it later.
