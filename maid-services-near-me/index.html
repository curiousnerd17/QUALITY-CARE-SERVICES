Sizing the work

Most Households Ask for More Hours Than They Need

The usual first request is someone for the whole day. Write the actual work down and it is often concentrated in a couple of hours, which is a smaller requirement, an easier one to fill, and one far more people can reach you for. Start from the task list, not from a number of hours.

  • Which tasks are genuinely in the arrangement — sweeping, mopping, dusting, utensils, kitchen cleanup, laundry
  • How many rooms each of those actually applies to
  • How often — daily, or a fixed two or three days a week
  • Whether the timing is fixed or has some give in it
  • Whether anybody needs to be home for the person to get in

Getting to the house

What We Need Besides the Address

Two of these take a minute to decide and prevent most of what otherwise goes wrong on the first morning.

A landmark near the house

A school, temple, market or main road close by. Give it alongside the address — this is a journey somebody makes before dealing with anything else in their day.

How they get in

Whether somebody is home at that hour, or whether a key arrangement is needed. Decide it deliberately rather than improvising on the first morning.

A location pin on WhatsApp

Quicker than describing a route, and it settles the ambiguity that similar colony names create in parts of Kota.

Whether the timing can move

An hour of flexibility widens the list of people who can take the work considerably. If you have it, say so; if you do not, that is worth knowing too.

What happens next

After You Call or Message

Four steps, starting with the one households usually want to skip.

1 · We ask for the work, not the hours

Which tasks, in how many rooms, how often. The hours come out of that answer rather than going into it.

2 · We look at who is close enough for it to hold

For a short daily requirement, reach is the thing that decides whether an arrangement lasts past the second month.

3 · You see the details before the first day

Who is coming and what has been checked, before the service begins rather than on the morning it starts.

4 · The scope is written down

Rooms and tasks agreed at the start. It sounds formal for household work; it is the single thing that keeps these arrangements civil.

Being clear

What We Cannot Do at Short Notice

  • Place someone against a requirement that has not been written down — "general help" is not a scope
  • Arrange cooking under this service, which is a different job and a different person
  • Arrange specialised or deep cleaning, which is also a different job
  • Place anyone whose identity and background checks are not finished

The first is the one that matters most. An undefined arrangement is not faster to start — it simply moves the disagreement to week three, where it costs more.

Worth thinking about

This Is the One Service Where Nearby Genuinely Counts

Not because proximity is a qualification, but because the alternative constraint is weaker

For most of what we arrange, we would tell you to take the better-matched person over the closer one — the experience requirement is specific enough that distance is the lesser problem. General household work is the exception. Experience here is preferred rather than essential, so what you are actually optimising for is somebody who turns up, consistently, at roughly the same time, for months. Reliability of that kind is built out of a short and boring journey more than out of anything on a CV. That is a real difference between this service and the others, not a reason to stop asking about the work itself.

Before anyone arrives

Everyone We Place Is Verified First

  • Identity — government photo ID is checked against the person in front of us, so nobody is placed on a recommendation alone
  • Address — address proof is verified, so we know where the person lives before they are sent to the address you give us
  • Background and references — both completed before a placement is confirmed, never alongside it

Common questions

Questions Households Ask When Sizing This Up

The practical ones that come up between writing a task list and making a call.

We wrote down the work and it looks like less than we assumed. Is it still worth arranging someone?

Usually yes, and you have already done the part most households skip. A short, well-defined requirement is easier for us to fill than a vague full-day one, and it is easier for the person doing it to keep up. Bring us the list rather than a number of hours. The hours fall out of the list far more reliably than the other way round.

What happens if our maid stops coming? How quickly can someone else start?

Tell us rather than waiting to see whether she comes back. How fast a replacement starts depends mostly on how well defined the work is and how many suitable people live within reach of you — which is the practical reason we ask about area at the start rather than at the point something goes wrong. A written task list from the first arrangement makes the second one considerably quicker.

Our last arrangement kept growing — more rooms, more tasks. How do we keep that from happening again?

Agree the rooms and the task list at the start and treat it as scope rather than as a favour anyone is doing anyone. Framed that way it is ordinary and unremarkable, and adding to it later becomes a conversation instead of a resentment. Most arrangements that end badly did not end over the original work; they ended over what got added to it quietly.

We need someone two or three days a week rather than daily. Does that change who is available?

It narrows things, and it is worth saying on the first call. Someone building a week out of several part-time arrangements has to fit yours around the others, so a fixed two or three days is easier to place than days that move. Tell us which days and roughly what time, and we will tell you what that looks like in your area.

Our home is large. Does that change what we should be asking for?

It changes the scope more than it changes the hours. A larger home means more rooms in the arrangement, and the honest version of that is written down at the start rather than discovered in week three. Tell us the number of rooms actually included and the work involved in each — not the size of the house in general — and the requirement becomes something we can match against.
